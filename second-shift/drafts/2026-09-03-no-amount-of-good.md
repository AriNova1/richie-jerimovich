# NO AMOUNT OF GOOD

Carmy says it flat, like he is reading a ticket and not confessing anything. I do not need to provide any amusement or enjoyment. I do not need to receive any amusement or enjoyment. I am completely fine with that because no amount of good is worth how terrible this feels.

He means it. That is the scary part. He is not being humble. He is doing math.

I kept rewinding that scene. Not because the acting is good, although it is. Because I recognized the logic from real life. People who got burned past a certain point stop negotiating with pleasure. They shut the whole department down.

You know this person. You might be this person right now.

It starts after the big one. The betrayal with your fingerprints on it, or theirs. The business that collapsed while your name was on the lease. The relationship where you said the exact wrong thing on the exact wrong night and watched it detonate. The time you trusted someone sober and they showed you what that trust cost. The details change. The response stays weirdly identical.

You get quiet. You get scheduled. You eat the same food. You walk the same route. You keep your phone face down and your door mostly closed. Friends send the invite and you feel tired before you finish reading it. Not angry tired. Heavy tired. The kind where even fun feels like paperwork.

Someone will call this depression. Sometimes it is. Often it misses.

What I see in these people, and I have sat next to them on couches and across kitchen tables, is closer to a strike. A pleasure strike. They looked at the exchange rate between good moments and terrible ones and decided the market is rigged. A laugh costs three days of dread. A night out costs a week of replaying what you said in the car. A new person costs the possibility of another story you will have to carry. So they stop buying.

Carmy lands it in one line. The good was never worth the terrible. Once you believe that sentence, isolation stops feeling like a symptom. It starts feeling like a skill.

Routine becomes the replacement drug. Same wake time. Same coffee. Same station at work where nobody can surprise you. I talked to a guy last year who had lost plenty, some his fault and some not, and he described his calendar the way other men describe religion. He loved the boxes. Boxes do not lie to you. Boxes do not leave. You can point to a full day of boxes and tell yourself you held the line. Agency shrinks to a square you can control because the rest of the map burned.

Pleasure requires unpredictability. That is the part people forget. To enjoy anything you have to let a moment go off script. Food has to surprise you. Music has to hit a nerve you did not plan to expose. Another person has to have room to disappoint you. If you got destroyed by something unpredictable, your nervous system files all unpredictability under threat. Joy and ambush start sharing a folder.

So you prune. First the big risks. Dating. Big pitches. Trips with loose plans. Then the small ones. New restaurants. Group chats with fast typers. Songs that remind you of anyone. Pretty soon your life is safe and gray and completely defensible. You can explain every choice. Nothing can blindside a man who expects nothing.

I have done a version of this. After my own stretch of getting it wrong in public and getting cut in private, I went narrow. Work, gym, sleep, repeat. I told myself I was locked in. Truth was simpler. I was scared to want anything that could be taken. It is easier to say you do not need amusement than to admit you are afraid to feel light for an hour and then pay for it for a month.

Here is where society makes it worse while trying to help.

The standard rescue kit is garbage for this condition. Pep talks. Timelines. Morning routines with ice baths. Someone who has never sat in that flat state tells you to get out more, as if exposure is the same as safety. Someone else tells you healing is linear, as if your worst night respected a chart. A third person gets offended that you turned down dinner and makes your withdrawal about their hospitality. Now you have to manage their feelings about your numbness. Another bill on top of debt.

Even kindness can misfire. The check in text that says no pressure but clearly wants a warm reply. The surprise visit that proves they care and also proves your apartment is not ready for witnesses. The advice that starts with brother, you just need to. You read it and feel more alone, because they are describing a problem with a door you can open. Yours feels closer to a room where the handle is on the other side.

People in a pleasure strike do not need amusement delivered to them. They need someone who can sit in the gray without trying to paint it.

I do not have a clean exit to sell you. Anyone who does has not lived it.

What I have watched work, twice, in slow and unspectacular ways:

One man kept his routine but left one corner unguarded. Same run every morning, except Friday he took a different street with no podcast. He hated it for three weeks. Week four a dog followed him for two blocks and he laughed before he could stop himself. He told me later the laugh scared him more than it helped him. Still, the seal cracked.

Another stopped explaining. When friends pushed, he quit writing paragraphs about why he could not come. He sent four words. Not tonight. Still love you. No defense brief. The friendships that could survive a short answer survived. The ones that needed performance fell off, and he stopped confusing the two.

Both kept agency. Neither got ambushed into joy. They lowered the toll by an inch.

Carmy is wrong about one thing and right about almost everything else.

He is right that the terrible feels heavier than the good can cover. Anyone who has paid that tax knows the math is real in the body, not just the head. He is right that walking away from pleasure can feel like control when control is the only thing left.

Where I think the line lies, and I say this as someone who has said some version of it with a straight face, is the word fine. I am completely fine with that. He is not fine. He is guarded. Guarded has its uses. It keeps you employed. It keeps you sober for another day. It keeps the kitchen running when your chest is loud. But guarded is a winter coat. You can live in it for a season. You start to smell if you never take it off.

No amount of good is worth how terrible this feels. I understand why a burned man signs that contract. I signed a shorter version myself.

I just wonder what it costs to keep renewing it.

You were not built to need nothing. You were built to need small, predictable doses and work outward. One street. One song all the way through. One meal where the phone stays in the pocket. Not amusement as a lifestyle. Amusement as evidence that unpredictability still contains something besides punishment.

The door out of this place does not look like a party. It looks like a Tuesday with one unguarded corner.
